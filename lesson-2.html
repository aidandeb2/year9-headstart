Multiplying algebraic terms

  1. Welcome to Headstart algebra. This is Lesson 2: multiplying algebraic terms. By the end of this video, you'll be able to write terms in index form, and multiply terms using the index law.
  2. First, index form. When the same letter is multiplied by itself, we can write it with an index, also called a power. a³ means a × a × a.
  3. The small raised number, 3, is the index. It tells us how many times the base is multiplied. The base is the letter, a.
  4. And a letter with no index has an index of one. So a is the same as a¹.
  5. To write in index form, count each letter. In 3 × a × a × a × b, the letter a appears three times, and b once. So we write 3a³b. The number goes first, then the letters in alphabetical order.
  6. Try another. 2 × x × x × y × y × y has two x's and three y's. So it is 2x²y³.
  7. Before you multiply anything, check the sign. A positive times a positive is positive.
  8. A negative times a negative is also positive. Two negatives make a positive.
  9. A positive times a negative is negative.
  10. And a negative times a positive is negative too. So: same signs give a positive, and different signs give a negative.
  11. Now let's multiply terms. We'll use this example: 3a³b × −2a²b². There are three steps.
  12. Step one: write each term in expanded form. Every letter is written out in full, one at a time.
  13. Step two: group the coefficients together, and group the matching letters. That's 3 and −2, then five a's, and three b's.
  14. Step three: write it back in index form. 3 × −2 is −6, five a's is a⁵, and three b's is b³. So the answer is −6a⁵b³.
  15. Writing everything out takes a long time, so here is a shortcut. Multiply the coefficients, and add the indices of matching letters.
  16. For example, a³ × a⁴ is a × a × a times a × a × a × a, which is seven a's. That's a⁷. We simply added the indices: 3 + 4 = 7.
  17. Letters that appear in only one term are just copied across. So 3ab × 2c is 6abc.
  18. Be careful about what an index applies to. An index only applies to the number or letter immediately to its left.
  19. In 4gh³, the 3 only applies to h. So it's 4 × g × h × h × h.
  20. But in (4gh)³, the brackets make the 3 apply to everything inside. So 4 is cubed too: 4³ is 64, so we get 64g³h³.
  21. And for a power of a power, multiply the indices. (x²)³ is x⁶.
  22. Three helpful hints. First, use brackets when an index should apply to more than one thing.
  23. Second, always write the coefficient first, then the letters in alphabetical order. So b × 3 × a becomes 3ab.
  24. And third, always check the sign first. Two negatives make a positive.
  25. You should now be able to write repeated multiplication in index form, work out the sign of a product, multiply terms by multiplying coefficients and adding indices, and tell the difference between 4gh³ and (4gh)³.   1. In this video we'll work through seven worked examples from Lesson 2. For each one, pause the video and try the problem yourself first. Then follow the think and write steps to check your thinking.
  2. Example one: writing terms in index form. Simplify 2 × a × a × a, and −9 × b × a × a.
  3. For part a, count how many times a appears. Three times. So 2 × a × a × a is 2 × a³, which is 2a³.
  4. For part b, the letter a appears twice and b once. Write the number first, then the letters in alphabetical order. So we get −9a²b.
  5. Example two: multiplying terms. Simplify 4ab × 3c.
  6. Write each term in expanded form: 4 × a × b × 3 × c.
  7. Group the numbers first, then the letters: 4 × 3 × a × b × c.
  8. Multiply the numbers. 4 × 3 is 12. So the answer is 12abc.
  9. Example three: a negative coefficient. Simplify 5a² × −a⁴.
  10. The term −a⁴ has an invisible coefficient of minus one. So we can write it as −1a⁴.
  11. Multiply the coefficients: 5 × −1 is −5. Then add the indices: 2 + 4 is 6. The answer is −5a⁶.
  12. Example four: using the shortcut. Simplify −2p²q³ × 7qp⁵.
  13. First, multiply the coefficients. −2 × 7 is −14.
  14. Now add the indices. For p, that's 2 + 5, which is 7. For q, it's 3 + 1, which is 4, because q on its own has an index of one.
  15. Put it together. The answer is −14p⁷q⁴.
  16. Example five: brackets with an index. Expand (3x²)⁴.
  17. The 4 applies to everything inside the brackets. So we get 3⁴ × (x²)⁴.
  18. 3⁴ is 3 × 3 × 3 × 3, which is 81. And for (x²)⁴, we multiply the indices. 2 × 4 is 8. So the answer is 81x⁸.
  19. Example six: finding a missing factor. Complete 4a × □ = 8ab².
  20. What do you multiply 4 by to get 8? 4 × 2 is 8.
  21. The letter a is already on both sides, so we don't need another one. But b² is missing from the left.
  22. So the missing part is 2b². Check by multiplying: 4a × 2b² is 8ab². It works.
  23. Example seven: the area of a rectangle. The length is 6x and the width is 8y. Find the area.
  24. Area equals length times width. So we multiply 6x × 8y, which is 6 × 8 × x × y. That's 48xy.
  25. That's the end of the seven worked examples. Now try the questions in your lesson booklet, and remember to show your working in the boxes. Always check the sign first!

The big idea

Multiply the coefficients. Add the indices.
  1. Check the sign first: same signs give +, different signs give −.
  2. Multiply the coefficients.
  3. Add the indices of each matching pronumeral. Write the coefficient first, then the letters in alphabetical order.
Example: Simplify 3a3b × −2a2b2
Sign and coefficients3 × −2 = −6
Add the indicesa: 3 + 2 = 5 · b: 1 + 2 = 3
Write it back−6a5b3
Remember: Letters that appear in only one term are just copied across: 3ab × 2c = 6abc.
