  1. Welcome to Headstart algebra. This is Lesson 5: expanding. By the end of this video, you'll be able to expand brackets using the distributive law.
  2. Expanding means removing the brackets by multiplying. The term outside the brackets is shared with every term inside. This is called the distributive law.
  3. Here's an example with numbers. 7(30 + 2) is 7 × 30 + 7 × 2, which is 210 + 14, or 224. In algebra, a(b + c) is a × b + a × c, which is ab + ac.
  4. The area model shows why this works. Imagine a rectangle that is 7 tall, and a + 2 wide.
  5. We can split it into two smaller rectangles: one that is 7 by a, and one that is 7 by 2.
  6. The areas are 7 × a, which is 7a, and 7 × 2, which is 14.
  7. The whole rectangle is 7(a + 2). The two parts add to 7a + 14.
  8. So 7(a + 2) equals 7a + 14.
  9. Here's how to expand. Let's try −7p(3q − 4). The term outside the brackets is negative, which makes signs tricky.
  10. Step one: multiply each term inside the brackets by the term outside. When the outside term is negative, put each product in brackets to protect the signs. So we get −7p × 3q, plus the bracket −7p × −4.
  11. Step two: simplify. −7p × 3q is −21pq. And −7p × −4 is plus 28p, because two negatives make a positive. The answer is −21pq + 28p.
  12. With two brackets, expand each one, then collect like terms. For 5(a + 4) + 2(a + 7), we get 5a + 20 + 2a + 14.
  13. Collect the like terms: 5a + 2a is 7a, and 20 + 14 is 34. The answer is 7a + 34.
  14. And watch out for a minus sign in front of a bracket. −(a + 2) means −1 × (a + 2). So every sign inside flips. The answer is −a − 2.
  15. Three helpful hints. First, when you multiply by a negative term, use brackets to keep track of the signs.
  16. Second, show all your working, to avoid arithmetic and sign errors.
  17. And third, after expanding, always simplify. Simplify, simplify, simplify!
  18. You should now be able to use the distributive law with numbers and letters, expand when the outside term is negative, expand two brackets and collect like terms, and show an expansion using an area model. Next, watch the worked examples video, then try the questions in your booklet.

  1. In this video we'll work through seven worked examples from Lesson 5. For each one, pause the video and try the problem yourself first. Then follow the think and write steps to check your thinking.
  2. Example one: expanding by a whole number. Expand and simplify 6(a + 4), 2(3x − y), and 3(2x + 4y + 5).
  3. For part a, multiply each term inside by 6. 6 × a is 6a, and 6 × 4 is 24. So we get 6a + 24.
  4. For part b, the second term is negative, so keep the minus. 2 × 3x is 6x, and 2 × y is 2y. The answer is 6x − 2y.
  5. For part c, there are three terms inside, so multiply all three by 3. We get 6x + 12y + 15.
  6. Example two: expanding by a variable. Expand and simplify −7p(3q − 4), 2m(5m − 4n), and 5a(a − 3b − 5).
  7. For part a, put the second term in brackets, and remember that a negative times a negative is a positive. −7p × 3q is −21pq, and −7p × −4 is 28p. So we get −21pq + 28p.
  8. For part b, remember that m × m is m². 2m × 5m is 10m², and 2m × −4n is −8mn. The answer is 10m² − 8mn.
  9. For part c, multiply all three terms by 5a. 5a × a is 5a². 5a × −3b is −15ab. And 5a × −5 is −25a. So the answer is 5a² − 15ab − 25a.
  10. Example three: the area model. Show that 10(a + 3) = 10a + 30 by finding the area of each rectangle.
  11. Draw a rectangle 10 wide and a + 3 tall. Split it into a rectangle of height a, and one of height 3. Rectangle one has area 10 × a, which is 10a. Rectangle two has area 10 × 3, which is 30.
  12. The large rectangle is the sum of the two. So 10(a + 3) equals 10a + 30.
  13. Example four: two brackets, then collect. Simplify 3(x + 4) + 2(x − 1).
  14. Expand the first bracket. 3(x + 4) is 3x + 12.
  15. Expand the second bracket. 2(x − 1) is 2x − 2.
  16. Now add, and collect like terms. 3x + 2x is 5x, and 12 − 2 is 10. The answer is 5x + 10.
  17. Example five: subtracting a bracket. Simplify 3(d − 2) − (d + 8).
  18. The expression −(d + 8) means −1 × (d + 8). So every sign inside flips, and we get −d − 8.
  19. Expand the first bracket. 3(d − 2) is 3d − 6.
  20. Now collect like terms. 3d − d is 2d, and −6 − 8 is −14. The answer is 2d − 14.
  21. Example six: expand, then evaluate. Expand 5a(3a + 10), then evaluate when a = 4.
  22. Multiply both terms by 5a. 5a × 3a is 15a², and 5a × 10 is 50a.
  23. Now substitute a = 4. 15 × 4² is 15 × 16, which is 240. And 50 × 4 is 200. The total is 440.
  24. Check with the original. 5 × 4 is 20, and the bracket is 3 × 4 + 10, which is 22. 20 × 22 is also 440. It matches.
  25. Example seven: a word problem. In three years Emily will be twice as old as Chantelle. Chantelle is c years old now. Write an expression for Emily's age in three years.
  26. In three years, Chantelle will be c + 3.
  27. Emily will be twice that, so we write 2(c + 3).
  28. Expand it. 2 × c is 2c, and 2 × 3 is 6. So Emily's age is 2c + 6.

The big idea

Share the outside term with every term inside
  1. Multiply each term inside the brackets by the term outside.
  2. Simplify: multiply, then collect like terms.
  3. A minus in front of a bracket flips every sign inside: −(a + 2) = −a − 2.
Example: Simplify 3(d − 2) − (d + 8)
Expand the first bracket3(d − 2) = 3d − 6
Expand the second−(d + 8) = −d − 8 (the minus flips both signs)
Collect like terms3d − 6 − d − 8 = 2d − 14

Why it works: the area model. The big rectangle is made of two smaller ones.
